one proposition · one semagram

The whole sentence, written at once.

An original script on a published idea real: writing whose shapes carry meaning directly, with no spoken language underneath. Here a whole proposition is one ring illustrative. Who acts, what changes hands, when, whether it is denied and how the writer knows each set a part of the ring's form, and the ring is solved as one curve, so no stroke can be placed before it is known how the sentence ends real.

the key

Where each part of the meaning lives in the ring.

Most human writing is glottographic: an alphabet records the sounds of a spoken language, and Chinese characters record its words (logographic). Semasiographic writing records meaning with no spoken language underneath, as road signs and mathematical notation do; Sampson (1985) draws the line, and Chiang's heptapods write this way real. This script is semasiographic illustrative. Read it clockwise from the top: four role sectors and four slots share the full circle; an absent role takes no arc and a slot that carries a mark needs more room, so every element's position depends on every other. Inside a role sector the concept is six cells that swell out, lie flat or dip in illustrative.

a breakable codec

Read it back through ink.

The decoder sees only this grey page, 1400 by 840 pixels real. It finds the ring along 360 rays, tries every layout the script allows (128 for one ring) and keeps the one whose sectors read as words. Damage the ink by type, each in its own unit, or brush erasure and ink onto the page yourself, and watch where it fails. The solid curve is the repository's bench: 200 random propositions per level real.

    composition

    Two propositions, one semagram.

    A relation joins two rings into one figure illustrative. The bridge carries the relation by its shape: a flowing stroke for because, two crossing strokes for despite, an arch over for after, an arch under for before. A participant of the second proposition that is also in the first is written once: the second ring holds only a reference code, linked back by a hairline, and the reader fills it in real. The left ring holds the main proposition.

    simultaneous and sequential

    Written at once, or one stroke after another.

    On the left the script as designed: the arcs are shared out over the whole circle and the ring is the one curve that minimises its bending and its distance from every cell, so it grows from every point together real. On the right the same sentence by a pen that moves clockwise and commits as it goes illustrative: it gives each element its natural arc because it does not know what comes next, and it steers toward each cell with only a few degrees of lookahead. The numbers below each are computed here real.

    the idea underneath

    Light picks the path of least time.

    In the story a physicist explains Fermat's principle with a ray of light passing from air into water real. Seen causally, the ray reaches the surface and bends there by a local rule. Seen variationally, of all the paths between the two points it takes the one that is quickest as a whole, so the path is chosen knowing where it ends. Both views give the same ray: the crossing point found by minimising travel time and the one found from Snell's law alone are shown side by side real. Drag the two points; change the lower medium.

    evidence

    What is real here, and what is made up to show the mechanism.

    • [REAL] The idea. Ted Chiang, "Story of Your Life", first published in Starlight 2, ed. Patrick Nielsen Hayden (Tor, 1998); collected in Stories of Your Life and Others (Tor, 2002). In it the linguist Louise Banks calls the heptapods' writing semasiographic and its units semagrams, a writer must know a sentence's whole layout before the first stroke, inflections change a stroke's curvature or thickness, and Fermat's principle frames the simultaneous view. The film Arrival (2016) adapted the story; its logograms belong to the film's designers and nothing here is drawn from them.
    • [REAL] The term. Geoffrey Sampson, Writing Systems: A Linguistic Introduction (Stanford University Press, 1985), distinguishes semasiographic writing, which links marks to meaning without passing through a spoken language, from glottographic writing, which records a spoken language. Every full human script is glottographic; whether a semasiographic system could match a spoken language is the open question the story imagines answered.
    • [ILLUSTRATIVE] The script. Its layout, the six-cell radicals, the marks for time, mood, aspect, evidence and negation, the nested ring and the bridges are an original design for this lab. The lexicon of 322 concepts, each with a part of speech and a gloss, was chosen by hand for this lab; a noun outside it is written with a marked fallback radical and read back as "something". The grammar is open: any proposition its schema can hold becomes one semagram.
    • [REAL] A real semasiographic precedent. Charles K. Bliss, International Semantography: A Non-Alphabetical Symbol Writing Readable in All Languages (Sydney, 1949), later Blissymbolics, built a writing of meaning from combinable symbols; it is still used for communication by people who cannot speak. This script borrows nothing from it but the aim.
    • [REAL] The computation. The ring is the exact minimiser of the stated energy (a 360 by 360 symmetric system, solved by Cholesky factorisation). The decoder reads a raster, not the geometry; every round-trip count, field accuracy and breaking point on this page is measured in your browser when you look at it. The parser round-trips: for 3,000 random graphs g, parse(realise(g)) = g. All of this runs headless: node labs/assets/js/heptapod/selftest.mjs and node labs/assets/js/heptapod/bench.mjs.
    • [ILLUSTRATIVE] The sequential pen is a model of a writer who commits as they go (a damped tracker with four degrees of lookahead); the failure it shows is computed, the writer is invented.
    • [REAL] The optics. Refractive indices at 589.3 nm: air 1.000277 (dry air, 15 °C, 101.325 kPa; P. E. Ciddor, Applied Optics 35, 1566, 1996) and water 1.33335 (20 °C; M. Daimon and A. Masumura, Applied Optics 46, 3811, 2007). The least-time path is found by minimising travel time numerically and checked against Snell's law, n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2; see R. P. Feynman, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 26 (1963).
    • [REAL] The model route. "Ask the model" makes one call through the labs' shared route (your machine, your key, or the public keyless route, which allows one reply per 45 seconds); its JSON is validated against the schema, and the offline parser answers when no route does.
