the survey of every planet THE LAB · L212

cosmos · transit clocks

Every transiting planet is a clock. Some can no longer be read.

A planet that crosses its star does so on schedule. The schedule is published as one mid-transit time and a period, each with an error, and the error of the prediction grows with every orbit real. This page carries each published clock forward to any date, draws the planet as sharp as that clock allows illustrative, and checks older clocks against what later papers measured real.

This lab's earlier page, a survey of every confirmed planet, is still here.

    illustrative

    The window is the clock's own 1-sigma error plus what its stated time standard leaves unsaid. A barycentric date is turned into your clock to within about 5 seconds; a date whose scale is not stated can be 69 seconds further off, and a bare "JD" up to 10 minutes real. A time is shown to the nearest minute, or to the nearest second when its window is under a minute. Altitude ignores refraction.

    Lost means that the 1-sigma error of the planet's working clock is now longer than the transit itself real. The word is this lab's illustrative. A working clock is contradicted when another published solution of the same planet predicts today's transit more than 3 sigma away from it real.

    Honest Gaussian errors would put 68 % inside one sigma and 0.3 % beyond three real. A real test here is a planet's earliest dated solution carried to the mid-transit time of the latest paper that can test it: a different paper, published two years or more later, whose mid-time is ten or more orbits on and later than the day the first paper appeared. A test is left out when the two uncertainties together had by then reached a sixth of the orbit: a mid-time is never more than half an orbit from the nearest predicted transit, so nothing could have missed by three sigma. The test is lenient. A later paper usually fits the old transits again, the archive does not record which transits each used, and one catalogue is the newer paper in most of the tests. The misses are counted as they stand. A planet pulled by its neighbours does not keep a linear clock, and this page has not checked which of the misses are such planets: uncertain.

    evidence

    What is real here, and what is this lab's way of showing it.

    • [REAL] The data. NASA Exoplanet Archive, Planetary Systems table (doi:10.26133/NEA12) and Planetary Systems Composite Parameters (doi:10.26133/NEA13), transiting planets, retrieved on the date shown in plate c. The archive asks users to cite Christiansen et al. 2025, Planetary Science Journal 6, 186; that reference is given here as the archive gives it and was not read for this page. This research has made use of the NASA Exoplanet Archive, which is operated by the California Institute of Technology, under contract with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration under the Exoplanet Exploration Program. The two files are built by hand with labs/assets/data/exomania/build_ephemerides.py and are a snapshot: nothing here is fetched from the archive while you read.
    • [REAL] The clock. A linear ephemeris predicts transit n at T0 + nP, and with independent errors its uncertainty is the root of sigma_T0 squared plus n squared times sigma_P squared. The archive's tables give no covariance between the two errors, so none is used. A planet whose transits vary in time is not a linear clock, and this page treats it as one.
    • [ILLUSTRATIVE] The lamp and the word lost. The lamp is the transit's length divided by the 1-sigma error of the predicted mid-time; below one, this lab calls the clock lost. The planet drawn as an arc along its orbit is this lab's picture of that error. For an eccentric orbit the place between transits is the mean phase; the transit times do not depend on it.
    • [REAL] Which clock. The working clock of a planet is the published solution with the smallest error on the day the data was retrieved, among the solutions that another paper confirms and that no two agreeing papers contradict. The smallest error alone picks outliers: an independent review found 66 planets whose working clock every other solution contradicted, one of them by 4.6 days. The steps are written out in the build script. The archive's composite row is used only when its mid-time and period come from one paper: a row that mixes two papers pairs errors that do not belong together.
    • [REAL] The tally and its limits. The counts in plate c are computed in your browser from the same two files. Three faults were found in this lab's earlier tallies and are corrected here. The archive gives every ExoFOP row one publication date, July 2017, though their mid-times run to 2026, so rows without a true date take no part in any test, on plate a or plate c. The oldest clock of HD 209458 b has an error wider than its orbit, and every later paper was counted as landing inside it, so a test that could not fail is counted apart. Plate a counted as a test a paper published beside the clock's own, timing a transit from before either appeared, so plate a now uses the definition of plate c. node labs/assets/js/exoplanet/tests/overdue.test.mjs recomputes the counts from the committed files.
    • [REAL] Time. Barycentric and heliocentric dates are turned into UTC at Earth with the light-travel time across Earth's orbit and with TT minus UTC (69.184 s since 2017); J. Eastman, R. Siverd and B. S. Gaudi, PASP 122, 935 (2010), on why a mid-transit time must state its standard. The Sun's position, sidereal time and altitude follow J. Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed. (Willmann-Bell, 1998), chapters 12, 13 and 25, and the page's tests reproduce four of its worked examples (12.a, 12.b, 13.b and 25.a). The Sun stands in for the solar system's barycentre, which costs up to about 5 seconds.
    • [REAL] The two examples. HD 209458 b was the first planet seen to transit: D. Charbonneau et al., ApJ 529, L45 (2000), and G. W. Henry et al., ApJ 529, L41 (2000). WASP-12 b's period is shrinking: S. W. Yee et al., ApJ Letters 888, L5 (2020). A shrinking period is one way a linear clock runs wrong.
    • [REAL] Who keeps these clocks for observers. The ExoClock project and the Exoplanet Transit Database collect new timings and publish refined ephemerides. This page shows the state of the archive's tables and does not replace either.
    • [REAL] What stays in your browser. The planet you opened and, if you set one, your place (rounded to a hundredth of a degree) are kept in the labs' own store in this browser, and the privacy page can export or erase them. If you have allowed sharing of usage, the author receives which planet was opened, the year a drag on the record ended on, and whether a place was typed or taken from the device. The place itself is never sent. The page uses no model.
