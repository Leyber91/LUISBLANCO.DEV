    evidence · what is real here, and what is this lab's way of showing it

    • [REAL] The data. NASA Exoplanet Archive, Planetary Systems table (doi:10.26133/NEA12) and Planetary Systems Composite Parameters (doi:10.26133/NEA13), transiting planets, retrieved on the date in the title block. The archive asks users to cite Christiansen et al. 2025, Planetary Science Journal 6, 186; that reference is given here as the archive gives it and was not read for this page. This research has made use of the NASA Exoplanet Archive, which is operated by the California Institute of Technology, under contract with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration under the Exoplanet Exploration Program. The two files are built by hand with labs/assets/data/exomania/build_ephemerides.py and are a snapshot: nothing here is fetched from the archive while you read.
    • [REAL] The clock. A linear ephemeris predicts transit n at T0 + nP, and with independent errors its uncertainty is the root of sigma_T0 squared plus n squared times sigma_P squared. The archive's tables give no covariance between the two errors, so none is used. A planet whose transits vary in time is not a linear clock, and this page treats it as one.
    • [REAL] Kept. A clock is drawn as kept while the 1-sigma error of its predicted time is a twelfth of the transit's length or less. That line is the target the ExoClock project sets for the planets ESA's Ariel mission will observe: A. Kokori et al., "ExoClock Project: an open platform for monitoring the ephemerides of Ariel targets with contributions from the public", Experimental Astronomy (2021), doi:10.1007/s10686-020-09696-3; the project's site states the target as one-twelfth of the transit duration (read 14 November 2026). ExoClock evaluates it for 2029; this page evaluates it on the date shown.
    • [ILLUSTRATIVE] Lost, slipping and the lamp. Below the target a clock is called slipping here, and once its error is longer than the transit itself it is called lost: both words and the second line are this lab's. Drawing a planet as a light that is gold while its clock is kept, and brighter the better it is kept, is this lab's picture of the transit's length divided by that error.
    • [REAL] The sky. Each lamp stands at its star's right ascension and declination from the archive, on Hammer's equal-area map. The ecliptic and the plane of the Galaxy are computed, the second with the north galactic pole at 12h 51.4m, +27.13° (J2000). The lens is a flat patch 18° across centred on the Kepler field. The K2 count is of planets the archive names K2 or EPIC, and its leader points at the 12° cell of the sky that holds the most of them.
    • [ILLUSTRATIVE] The sky before today. On a past date a planet is drawn by the best clock known then: a paper counts from the day it appeared, and a row the archive gives no true date counts from its own mid-transit time, the earliest it can have been measured. A later paper takes over when its error on that day is the smaller. The archive records what was published, not what an observer used, so this is a reconstruction: a clock shown as lost in a past year may have been kept by timings the table does not hold.
    • [REAL] Which clock is the working clock. The published solution whose forecast of today's transit agrees best with the forecasts of the other papers, each pair scored by how far apart they are, discounted for their stated errors (the continuous ranked probability score; T. Gneiting and A. E. Raftery, J. Am. Stat. Assoc. 102, 359, 2007). The rule was chosen by a test, after two rules chosen by argument failed independent review: hide each planet's latest paper, let a rule pick from the earlier ones, and measure the gap to the hidden paper. Over 3,462 planets this rule has the smallest median gap, 19.7 minutes, and the fewest confident misses. The same test shows what no rule mends: for about one planet in ten, no earlier clock comes within three hours of the latest paper. backtest_clock.py, beside the build script, prints the table.
    • [REAL] Where each paper found the transit. Sheet b draws every other published mid-time minus what the clock in use predicts for that orbit, by year: the chart observers call observed minus calculated. A paper is gold when it lies inside its own error and the clock's together.
    • [REAL] The tally and its limits. The counts in sheet d are computed in your browser from the same two files. A real test is a planet's earliest dated solution carried to the mid-transit time of the latest paper that can test it: a different paper, published two years or more later, whose mid-time is ten or more orbits on and later than the day the first paper appeared. Rows without a true date take no part. A test is left out when the two uncertainties together had reached a sixth of the orbit, where nothing can miss by three sigma. The test is lenient: a later paper usually fits the old transits again, the archive does not record which transits each used, and one catalogue is the newer paper in most of the tests. A planet pulled by its neighbours does not keep a linear clock, and this page has not checked which of the misses are such planets: uncertain. node labs/assets/js/exoplanet/tests/overdue.test.mjs recomputes the counts from the committed files.
    • [REAL] The transit as light. The dip is limb-darkened and central, from the archive's depth and length. The heavy line is that dip averaged over the error of its predicted time: what a telescope pointed at the prediction would record on average.
    • [REAL] Time. Barycentric and heliocentric dates are turned into UTC at Earth with the light-travel time across Earth's orbit and with TT minus UTC (69.184 s since 2017); J. Eastman, R. Siverd and B. S. Gaudi, PASP 122, 935 (2010), on why a mid-transit time must state its standard. The Sun's position, sidereal time and altitude follow J. Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed. (Willmann-Bell, 1998), chapters 12, 13 and 25, and the page's tests reproduce four of its worked examples (12.a, 12.b, 13.b and 25.a). The Sun stands in for the solar system's barycentre, which costs up to about 5 seconds. A transit's window in sheet c is the clock's own 1-sigma error plus what its stated standard leaves unsaid: a date whose scale is not stated can be 69 seconds further off, and a bare "JD" up to 10 minutes. A time is shown to the nearest minute, or to the nearest second when its window is under a minute. Altitude ignores refraction.
    • [ILLUSTRATIVE] Up in the dark. The rule under sheet c is this lab's. Its half hour of sky before and after the transit follows the tool observers plan with, the TESS Transit Finder (astro.swarthmore.edu/transits, read 14 November 2026), which asks for out-of-transit baseline on both sides and widens it by the ephemeris uncertainty. That tool also limits by the star's brightness and the dip's depth; this page gives both in the table and applies no limit. Until you set a place the night is drawn for Barcelona, as an example.
    • [REAL] The planet the sheet opens on. HD 209458 b was the first planet seen to transit: D. Charbonneau et al., ApJ 529, L45 (2000), and G. W. Henry et al., ApJ 529, L41 (2000).
    • [REAL] Who keeps these clocks for observers. The ExoClock project and the Exoplanet Transit Database collect new timings and publish refined ephemerides. This page shows the state of the archive's tables and does not replace either.
    • [REAL] What stays in your browser. The planet you opened and, if you set one, your place (rounded to a hundredth of a degree) are kept in the labs' own store in this browser, and the privacy page can export or erase them. If you have allowed sharing of usage, the author receives which planet was opened, the year a drag on the line of time ended on, whether a place was typed or taken from the device, and, as on every lab page, when the page was opened and left. The place itself is never sent. The page uses no model.
